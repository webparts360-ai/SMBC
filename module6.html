Module 6 · Day 2 · Data-Driven Decision Making

Integrating Data Analytics
into Managerial Practice

The previous modules gave you the tools. This module is about how to use them — in the decisions you make, the metrics you trust, the risks you monitor, and the AI recommendations your teams will soon be presenting to you.

Total duration 75 minutes

Module 6 Session guide — segments, learning outcomes & artefacts
Learning outcomes — by the end of this module participants will be able to
1

Explain why analytical capability at management level is a risk management obligation — not a technical nice-to-have — using the TD Bank and mortgage bank case as evidence.

2

Apply a four-question framework to evaluate whether any metric they currently track is persistent, predictive, controllable, and still valid.

3

Describe how analytics shifts risk and compliance management from periodic review to continuous signal monitoring — and name the managerial behaviours that shift requires.

4

Use a four-question readiness test to assess whether an AI or ML proposal is fit for purpose in their area — and identify the failure modes that most commonly undermine data-driven decisions.

Opening · 12–15 minutes
Two Banks, One Crisis — the story that frames the module
In 2006, a quantitative analyst built a model that could have saved billions. The analysis was correct. The warning was issued. The report was sent. Nothing happened — because the manager who received it could not engage with it. This story is not about analysis. It is about what happens when management lacks the capability to act on analysis it receives. It sets the question this entire module answers.

Segment 1 · 20 minutes
Metrics That Actually Predict Outcomes
Most management dashboards track what has happened. Very few track what is about to happen. This segment gives participants a four-question framework to tell the difference — tested against a live banking dashboard that mirrors what they open on a Monday morning.
The problem with most dashboards — overloaded and underused, describing the past rather than predicting the future
Vanity metrics and the DoSomething.org example — 1.5 million views, eight sign-ups, zero donations
The four-question test: persistent, predictive, controllable, still valid — applied to each metric in the banking dashboard
Planted traps: average credit score (the 2006 metric), branch staff turnover (the TD teller example), and NPL ratio (leading vs lagging)
Peer discussion exercise: participants test one metric from their own area and share one observation

Segment 2 · 15 minutes
Risk, Compliance & Continuous Signal Monitoring
Analytics does not just make risk management more accurate. It makes it faster. The shift from periodic review to continuous monitoring replaces the calendar with signals — and changes the manager's role from reviewer to responder. This segment makes that shift visible with a live credit deterioration timeline and a signal-to-action reference covering APAC regulatory expectations.
The shift from periodic to continuous — how the trigger changes from a date to a threshold breach
APAC regulatory context: MAS 626, HKMA AML-2, BSP, BNM — continuous monitoring is increasingly an obligation, not a best practice
Interactive timeline: an 18-month corporate credit deterioration showing the detection gap between periodic review (11-week lag) and continuous monitoring (3-day detection)
Signal-to-action reference: 13 specific signals across credit, transaction, and compliance — each with defined threshold and management decision
Self-assessment: which signals does your team monitor, which have a defined threshold, which have a defined response

Segment 3 · 20 minutes · No dedicated artefact — links to Module 7
AI & ML — A Decision Framework for Managers
Module 7 covers AI in banking in depth. This segment does not duplicate it. Its purpose is to give participants a tool — the ML Readiness Test — for evaluating any AI or ML proposal they receive. The framing is deliberately positioned as preparation for Module 7: participants leave with a framework to apply to the case studies they are about to see.
AI and ML as a category of analytical tool — when pattern complexity justifies ML over simpler analytics
The ML Readiness Test: Is the pattern too complex for a rule? Is there sufficient clean data? Is the cost of a wrong prediction acceptable? Can we act on the output?
Mentimeter scenario vote: SME credit scoring ML vs back-office document classification — which passes the test and why
The Harley-Davidson example — assessing ML fit in practice using a manufacturing use case transferable to banking
Transition to Module 7: "You now have a framework. The next session gives you the cases to apply it to."
Leads into Module 7 20 min · Discussion
Closing — 10 minutes
Close with three failure modes drawn from the HBR material — confirmation bias, overfitting and overconfidence, and vanity metrics — framed as practical diagnostics rather than academic concepts. Use a final Mentimeter poll: "Which of these three failure modes is most likely to affect your team right now?" The distribution closes the module on honest self-reflection rather than lecture. Transition directly into Module 7.
The thread across all four segments
Every segment in Module 6 asks the same question in a different context: when the analysis lands on your desk, what do you do with it? The case study makes the cost of the wrong answer concrete. The metrics exercise makes the framework practical. The risk segment makes the tempo visible. The AI segment makes the evaluation tool explicit.
Source thread: Davenport, T.H., "Keep Up with Your Quants," HBR Jul–Aug 2013 · Mauboussin, M., "The True Measures of Success," HBR Oct 2012 · HBR Guide to Data Analytics Basics for Managers
