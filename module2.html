Day 1 · Module 2

Benefits of Data-Driven
Decision Making

From confirmation bias to data culture — why organisations fail to use the data they have, and the stewardship practices that close the gap.

Duration 55 minutes
Format Virtual · Webex
Engagement Webex chat discussion
Assessment 5-question quiz
Day 1 Understanding the Importance of Data Analytics

Learning Outcomes

1

Recognise the gap between data availability and data-driven decision making in their own organisation.

2

Distinguish between HIPPO-driven and evidence-driven decision behaviours at the management level.

3

Articulate why data stewardship is the structural foundation of a data-driven culture — not an IT function.

4

Identify at least three data stewardship practices to assess or initiate within their own team this week.

Session Flow

0:00 – 0:05
The Uncomfortable Truth
No slides. The drunk driver analogy delivered as narrative: most organisations use analytics to confirm decisions already made — not to make them. Confirmation bias named explicitly before any framework is introduced.
0:05 – 0:15
HIPPO vs Data-Driven Decisions
Four banking scenarios on screen. Participants read silently for 30 seconds, then respond in Webex chat. HIPPO framed as a rational response to a broken environment — not a character flaw.
0:15 – 0:23
Group Reflection
Webex chat: "Without naming your institution — which of the four HIPPO scenarios feels most familiar?" Facilitator reads responses aloud, reflects patterns back to the group without attribution. Two-minute discussion before the Waze bridge.
0:23 – 0:27
The Waze Bridge
Return to the Waze story from Module 1. The contrast: data upstream vs downstream of the decision. Most banks use data to report on decisions already made — not to change today's decision.
0:27 – 0:30
Data as Privilege vs Data as Asset
The conceptual hinge. When data is treated as a private resource — hoarded, inconsistently defined, unavailable at the moment of decision — experience and seniority will always win. Stewardship is the structural response.
0:30 – 0:50
Seven Data Stewardship Practices
Each practice: description, practical example, and one test question participants can apply in their own team. Approx 2.5 min per practice. Facilitator pauses for group reaction after practices 1, 3, and 6.
0:50 – 0:55
Module Close & Quiz
The single self-assessment question: what would a new analyst find? 30 seconds of silence. Personal commitment to one practice. Five-question consolidation quiz. Bridge to Module 3.

The Opening Frame

The Pattern
Decision first. Data second.
A senior leader has an instinct or a political commitment. The decision is made — informally, over lunch. The analytics team is then asked to produce numbers that support it. The data's job is not to inform. It is to validate.
This is confirmation bias. And it is so common it has become invisible.
The Analogy
The Drunk Driver
Makes it home safely. Concludes: the process was sound. Will do it again. The outcome was good because of luck — not because of judgement. And next time, the luck may not hold.
A good outcome tells you almost nothing about the quality of the decision that produced it.

HIPPO vs Data-Driven — Four Banking Scenarios

Read the table. Think — not about which organisation this describes, but about which response feels more familiar in your own experience. HIPPO = Highest Paid Person's Opinion.

Situation HIPPO Response Data-Driven Response
NPL ratio rises in one segment I know this book — it is the relationship managers. Tighten the approval criteria. Let us understand what drove it before we change criteria. Is it vintage, product, geography, or something we have not looked at?
New product proposal lands We tried something like this in 2019. It did not work. What does the data tell us about why 2019 failed? Are those conditions still in place today?
Dashboard shows declining customer activity The dashboard is wrong. Our relationship managers say the pipeline is strong. Let us reconcile the two sources before the next credit committee. If they disagree, one of them is wrong — we need to know which.
Model flags elevated risk in a portfolio The model has always been conservative. We have seen this before and nothing happened. When did we last validate this model? Let us stress-test the assumption before we dismiss it.
Key Point

HIPPO behaviour is a rational response to an environment where data is not trusted, not timely, or not available at the moment of decision. The HIPPO is not the disease. It is the symptom. The disease is how the organisation treats data.

Data as Privilege vs Data as Asset

Data as Privilege
Access rationed by seniority or department
Sharing requires justification and approval
Different teams maintain their own versions of the same dataset
Two reports show different numbers for the same metric
Data requests go through a queue
Documentation protects the owner — not the user
Data as Asset
Maintained, documented, governed — like the loan book
Access governed by decision rights — not politics
Single agreed definition of every key metric
"Where does this number come from?" has a clear answer anyone can give
Quality is measured and reported proactively
Shared by default unless there is a specific reason to restrict
The Core Framing
Data stewardship is not an IT programme. It is a management commitment.
Organisations that treat data as an asset have not made a larger technology investment than those that do not. They have made a different choice about what data is for. And that choice starts in this room.

Seven Data Stewardship Practices

For each practice: does this exist in your organisation? Partly? Not at all? Hold the rating — we will return to it at the close.

1
The Business Glossary
Agree on what your key words mean — before the next meeting where two reports show different numbers
A living document — owned by the business, not IT — recording the agreed definition of every key metric: how it is calculated, who owns the definition, when last reviewed, and which system is the authoritative source. Start with the ten metrics in your most important management report.
Example One APAC bank found its retail and wholesale divisions had been calculating cost-to-income ratio differently for three years. Retail included depreciation; wholesale did not. Both were presenting to the same board without anyone noticing.
Test Question If a new analyst asked for the definitive calculation of your three most important KPIs, could anyone give them a single consistent answer?
2
Named Data Ownership
Every dataset has a named human owner — a business-side manager, not a system
A named business-side manager — not a technologist — responsible for a dataset's accuracy, documentation, and fitness for use. Their four questions: What does this data represent? How is it collected? How often is it updated? Who is authorised to use it and for what? Add a data owner field to your model risk register and regulatory reporting framework.
Example If a dataset feeds a regulatory submission and it has no named owner, that is a material governance gap. MAS and HKMA examiners will ask — and the answer cannot be "IT owns it."
Test Question For your three most important management reports — who is accountable if the numbers are wrong? Can you name them right now?
3
Data Lineage
Know where your numbers come from — and be able to show your working to a regulator
The documented trail of how a number was produced: source system, transformations applied, business rules used. Three questions before presenting any significant figure to a committee, board, or regulator: Where did this come from? What assumptions or filters were applied? What would change this number if the assumptions were different?
Example During a MAS inspection, a bank was asked to show calculation methodology for a ratio in its ICAAP submission. The number was correct — but lineage documentation could not be produced in the required format. The regulator issued a finding. Not for the number. For the absence of documentation.
Test Question Pick one number from your last board presentation. Can you trace it back to its source system through every transformation in under five minutes?
4
Role-Based Access Tiers
Access governed by decision rights and role — not by seniority or relationship
Relationship managers cannot access credit model scores for their own portfolios. Branch managers cannot see their own churn data. Meanwhile the ED has access to everything and uses none of it. Access should be provisioned to anyone who needs it to do their job, with appropriate controls for genuinely sensitive data. The test: take any dataset that requires a special request to access and ask whether the restriction is there for a documented reason.
Example A regional bank found its relationship managers spent an average of four hours per week requesting data they needed for client reviews. When access was provisioned by role rather than request, that time was recaptured for client-facing work. The data did not change. The access policy did.
Test Question Is there a dataset your team regularly requests that should be available by default? What is stopping it?
5
Data Quality as a Managed Metric
Measure and report data quality proactively — not only when something goes wrong
For each material dataset: completeness rate, error rate, age of the most recent record, known population of missing values. A data quality dashboard alongside the standard management information pack — green, amber, red on completeness, timeliness, and consistency — changes behaviour upstream because source teams know their quality is visible to management.
Example Banks that introduced quality dashboards found source data quality improved without a single governance intervention. Teams knew their quality would be visible to management, and the discipline of producing the dashboard changed what people paid attention to.
Test Question If you opened your most important dashboard right now, could you tell me how fresh the data is and how complete it is? Or do you assume it is fine until a number looks wrong?
6
The Sharing Default
Data is open unless there is a specific reason to close it — not closed unless someone makes the case to open it
Organisations with strong data cultures invert the restriction default. Data is shared unless there is a specific documented reason not to: regulatory restriction, client confidentiality, commercial sensitivity. The burden of justification falls on restriction, not on access. Most organisations find the genuinely restricted category is significantly smaller than assumed — often less than 20% of the total data estate.
Example One bank completing this categorisation exercise found 80% of its restricted data was restricted not for policy reasons, but because nobody had thought to open it. No security review was required. No policy change. Just a deliberate decision to ask whether each restriction was justified.
Test Question Think of the last time someone in your team could not access data they needed. Was the restriction there for a genuine reason — or because nobody had thought to remove it?
7
The Decision Post-Mortem
Use data to learn from decisions — not just to justify them
Ninety days after any significant decision: what data was available at the time, what assumptions were made, what the outcome revealed, and what the learning is. The goal is to improve the process — not identify the culprit. The most telling indicator of a data culture is what happens after a decision goes wrong. In low-maturity cultures, bad outcomes trigger blame cycles that teach people to be careful about what data they surface before a decision.
Example A credit team running quarterly post-mortems on NPL events identified three systematic predictive patterns within two years — patterns invisible to individual relationship managers but visible across the portfolio. The post-mortem process did not change the data. It changed what the team looked for.
Test Question Think of the last significant decision in your area that did not go as planned. Was there a structured review of what the data said at the time?

Key Takeaways

1
Confirmation bias is structural — not personal
The drunk driver and the HIPPO are symptoms of an environment where data is not trusted, not accessible, and not consistently defined. Change the environment and the behaviour changes.
2
The gap is upstream vs downstream
Waze works because data arrives before the decision. Most banks use data to report on decisions already made. The competitive advantage is in closing that sequence gap.
3
Data stewardship is a management commitment — not an IT programme
Seven practices. Any one of them can be started at the team level this week without a budget approval or an enterprise mandate. Pick the one most absent from your team.
4
Pick one. Start there.
Not all seven. The one practice most absent from your team and most within your control to change. That is the commitment to leave this module with.
