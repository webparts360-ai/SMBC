Module 6 · Day 2 · Opening Case Study

Two Banks, One Crisis
When the analysis was right and the manager wasn't listening

In 2006, a quantitative analyst produced a model that could have saved billions. The analysis was correct. The warning was issued. Nothing happened. This case study examines why — and what it means for how you engage with data in your role.

Source Harvard Business Review
Author Thomas H. Davenport
Programme Data-Driven Decision Making
Part 1 What happened — and why it matters to you
01
The Setup · 2006
The analyst who was right
A large US bank. A mortgage portfolio of $38bn. A quant team running risk models.

In 2006, a senior quantitative analyst at a large US bank built a probability-of-default model across the firm's residential mortgage book. The model incorporated credit scores, loan-to-value ratios, debt-to-income levels, origination vintage, and regional house price trends. The output was unambiguous: a significant portion of the portfolio was at serious risk of default — particularly if house prices stopped rising.

The analyst did what analysts do. He packaged his findings — regression outputs, scenario tables, confidence intervals — and sent the report to the head of the mortgage business. He had done his job.

Direct quote — the analyst, speaking to HBR professor Thomas Davenport
"I don't know why we didn't get the mortgages off our books. I had a model strongly indicating that a lot of them wouldn't be repaid, and I sent it to the head of our mortgage business."
02
The Failure
The manager who couldn't engage
The report landed. Nothing happened. The bank lost billions in bad loans during the 2008–2009 financial crisis.

When Davenport later asked the mortgage head why he had ignored the report, the answer revealed not one failure but three:

Direct quote — the head of mortgage business
"If the analyst showed me a model, it wasn't in terms I could make sense of. I didn't even know his group was working on repayment probabilities."
Two sentences. Two catastrophic failures packed inside them.
1

He couldn't read the output. The report was presented as a statistical model — regression coefficients, probability distributions, model fit statistics. The business head lacked the analytical literacy to extract the business implication from it.

2

He didn't know the work existed. There was no working relationship between this manager and the analytics function. No regular briefings, no shared framing, no context in which a risk report could land.

3

He didn't ask for clarification. Faced with something he couldn't understand, his response was to disengage — not to ask questions, not to seek a simpler explanation, not to escalate the uncertainty.

03
The Contrast · Toronto-Dominion Bank
The bank that ran the numbers
Same market. Same crisis. Entirely different outcome.

TD Bank's CEO at the time, Ed Clark, held a PhD in economics. More importantly, he created a culture where managers were expected to understand the mathematics behind any financial product the bank depended on. Analytics was not something a separate team did elsewhere — it was embedded in how decisions were made at every level.

When the risk signals emerged, TD's leadership could read them, interrogate them, and act. The bank avoided the riskiest structured products and exited others before the crisis hit. Clark's own summary of the culture: "Nobody ever accuses us of not running the numbers."

The question this raises for you

Both banks had access to the same data and the same analytical tools. The difference was entirely in how management engaged with it. As VP and ED-level decision-makers at SMBC, you are the mortgage head in this story — not the analyst. The question is: when the model lands on your desk, what do you do with it?

Facilitator note — title
The title contains no business signal. "Probabilistic Default Modelling" tells the reader nothing about what was found or what action is needed. Compare to the executive version's title: "Mortgage Portfolio: Significant Default Risk Identified."
1. Executive abstract
This report presents findings from a logistic regression-based probability of default (PD) model applied to the firm's residential mortgage portfolio (n = 184,320 active loans, gross exposure USD 38.4bn). The model incorporates FICO score, loan-to-value ratio (LTV), debt-to-income ratio (DTI), origination vintage, and geographic concentration as predictor variables. A secondary survival analysis (Cox proportional hazards) estimates time-to-default distributions conditional on macroeconomic scenario inputs. The null hypothesis — that current PD estimates remain within historical tolerance bands — is rejected at p < 0.001.
Facilitator note — abstract
"The null hypothesis is rejected at p < 0.001" is the analyst's way of saying the risk is real and statistically significant. A business head who does not know what a null hypothesis is will read straight past this sentence — the most important conclusion in the abstract — without registering it.
2. Model specification & variable selection
The primary model is specified as follows:
logit(PDi) = β₀ + β₁(FICOi) + β₂(LTVi) + β₃(DTIi) + β₄(Vintagei) + β₅(HPI_Δregional) + εi
Variable selection was performed via stepwise AIC minimisation (AIC = 41,882; BIC = 41,944). All predictors are statistically significant at the 5% level. Multicollinearity was assessed via VIF (all VIF < 4.2). The model achieves an AUC of 0.847 on the holdout validation set, indicating strong discriminatory power.

Facilitator note — regression table
This table contains the core finding: the "Vintage 2004–06" row shows an odds ratio of 1.848 — meaning 2004–2006 loans are nearly twice as likely to default. But the business head would need to know how to read an odds ratio to extract that. The number 1.848 sitting in a row of six numbers looks identical to 0.0341. Nothing marks it as the most important figure on the page.

Adverse scenarios incorporate mean-reversion and stress trajectories calibrated to historical regional downturns (1989–1993 Northeast; 1994–1997 California).

Facilitator note — the buried warning
This "Analyst Note" is the most important paragraph in the entire report. It says: a $2.8bn loss scenario is historically plausible and should not be dismissed. But it appears on page 3, after a regression formula, a coefficient table, and a scenario table. It is written in hedged, passive academic language. A non-quant business head reading quickly — or not reading at all — will never reach it.
4. Survival analysis: time-to-default
A Cox proportional hazards model was fitted to estimate time-to-default distributions, stratified by origination vintage. The hazard ratio for 2005–2006 vintage loans is 1.84 (95% CI: 1.61–2.10) relative to pre-2003 originations. Schoenfeld residual testing confirms proportional hazards assumption holds (p = 0.34).
h(t|x) = h₀(t) · exp(β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + ... + βₙxₙ)
Median time-to-default for loans currently 60+ DPD is estimated at 7.3 months (IQR: 4.1–12.8 months). The Kaplan-Meier survival curves for 2005–2006 vintage loans diverge significantly from earlier cohorts beyond month 18 of seasoning, consistent with ARM reset exposure concentrated in Q2–Q3 2007.
5. Model limitations & assumptions
The model assumes stationarity of the FICO-default relationship. This assumption may be violated if underwriting standards have deteriorated over the sample period in ways not captured by observable FICO scores. The HPI sensitivity parameter is calibrated on 1988–2005 data; extrapolation beyond –20% HPI decline is outside the model's training distribution. The model does not account for correlated defaults arising from systemic HPI shock.

Part 3 What this means for how you manage

This case is not primarily about communication — though communication failed. It is about the structural conditions that allowed a correct risk warning to be ignored at the cost of billions. Three lessons apply directly to your role.

01
Your job is to interrogate, not just receive
The mortgage head's core failure was passivity. He received a report he couldn't understand and did nothing. The manager's role is not to accept or reject analysis — it is to ask questions until the business implication is clear. "I don't understand this" is not a reason to disengage. It is a reason to demand a better explanation.
02
Know what assumptions are in the model
The entire 2008 crisis was embedded in one hidden assumption: that US house prices would always rise. That assumption was never stated explicitly, never challenged, and never monitored. Every model has assumptions. Your job is to ask: what would have to be true for this analysis to be wrong? And is that true right now?
03
Build the relationship before the crisis
The mortgage head had no working relationship with the analytics team. When the report arrived, it arrived cold — with no shared context, no prior conversations, no trust. TD Bank built something different: a culture where managers and analysts worked together continuously. By the time risk signals emerged, the relationship to act on them already existed.
The six questions every manager should ask of any analysis
1. What was the source of this data, and how was it collected?
2. What are the key assumptions behind this model? What would invalidate them?
3. What is the business implication — in plain language, in dollar terms?
4. What action does this analysis recommend, and by when?
5. What does this analysis not tell us? What is outside its scope?
6. If you had to act on this tomorrow, what would you do?
Source: Adapted from Davenport, T.H., "Keep Up with Your Quants," HBR July–August 2013
The TD Bank standard

At TD Bank under Ed Clark, if anyone in a management meeting suggested a new direction, they were pressed for data and analysis to support it. This was not a one-way street — it was a mutual expectation. Managers demanded rigour from analysts. Analysts were expected to communicate in business terms. The culture made both sides accountable. That is the standard this module asks you to consider for your own team.
