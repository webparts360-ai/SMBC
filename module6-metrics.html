Metrics That Actually
Predict Outcomes

Most management dashboards track what has happened. Very few track what is about to happen. This session gives you a four-question framework to tell the difference — and a real banking dashboard to test it on.

Part 1 Four questions every manager should ask of any metric

Management reporting is overloaded and underused. Most organisations track more metrics than they validate — numbers accumulate on dashboards for historical, political, or regulatory reasons, and are rarely re-examined. The result is a dashboard that describes the past with confidence and says almost nothing about the future. These four questions are designed to test whether a metric deserves the management attention it currently receives.

01
Is this metric persistent?
Persistence means the relationship between this metric and the outcome you care about is stable and consistent over time — not a single-quarter coincidence. If you went back through five years of data, would you consistently find that when this number moved, the outcome followed?
Warning sign: the metric was introduced recently, or has only been tracked for one or two reporting cycles. You are managing on faith, not evidence.
02
Is this metric predictive?
Predictive means a change in this metric today reliably precedes a change in the outcome — not just correlates with it after the fact. A metric can be highly correlated with an outcome without predicting it. Revenue and headcount correlate. Headcount does not predict revenue.
The test: does this number lead the outcome, or describe it once it has already happened? Lagging indicators confirm. Leading indicators enable action.
03
Is this metric within our control?
A metric your team cannot influence through management decisions is a measurement, not a management tool. You can monitor it. You cannot manage to it. Many organisations report external market indicators alongside internal performance metrics and treat them with equal urgency.
Distinction: monitoring external signals you cannot control — interest rates, HPI, sector stress — is right. The question is: what internal lever does this external signal tell you to pull?
04
Is this metric still valid?
Metrics have a shelf life. The conditions under which a metric was validated can change — and when they do, the metric stops being predictive, even though it continues appearing on the dashboard as if nothing has changed. This is the question most organisations forget to ask.
The 2006 lesson: average credit scores looked healthy. The metric was still being reported. But the underwriting conditions behind those scores had changed. Nobody asked question four. The model was no longer valid.
The practical standard — TD Bank under Ed Clark
If anyone in a management meeting suggested a new direction, they were pressed for data and analysis to support it. This was not a one-way street. Managers demanded rigour from analysts. Analysts were expected to communicate in business terms. Both sides were accountable. Nobody ever accused TD of not running the numbers.
The goal of this framework is not to make you an analyst. It is to make you a manager who cannot be misled by a metric that has stopped working.
Vanity metrics — a word of warning

A nonprofit ran a YouTube campaign in 2011. The video received 1.5 million views — twice their previous record. By every traffic measure, it was a success. Then they looked at the outcome data: eight people signed up. Zero donations. Views had no predictive relationship to the outcome they needed. The metric was measuring activity, not impact. Every dashboard contains at least one vanity metric. The four-question test will find it.
